## Feedback needs both people

- Explore the limits of describing feedback as a gift: giving it does not guarantee it is useful or understood.
- Develop the dance metaphor around listening, adjusting and responding, rather than delivering a verdict.
- Connect this to <ArticleLink slug="making-annual-reviews-valuable">making annual reviews valuable</ArticleLink> and the conversations that should happen throughout the year.

## Start with something specific

- Outline how to describe an observable action, its context and its impact without labelling a person's character.
- Add a clearly hypothetical example contrasting a vague judgement with a useful observation.
- Invite the other person's account before assuming intent.

## Listening does not require agreement

- Explore questions that clarify what happened and why perspectives differ.
- Distinguish understanding feedback from accepting every interpretation.
- Consider how the person giving feedback can change their own view.

## The power in the room

- Discuss why feedback upwards may feel riskier than feedback between peers.
- Explore timing, privacy, trust and a manager's responsibility to make disagreement possible.
- Avoid placing responsibility for an unsafe conversation entirely on its recipient.

## Agree what happens next

- Outline a practical sequence: observation, response, shared understanding, action and follow-up.
- Include commitments from both people, including support the manager can provide.
- Explain how to revisit the conversation without turning it into surveillance.

## A conversation worth continuing

- Close by returning to the metaphor: neither person can do all the listening or all the moving.
- Invite readers to reflect on what makes feedback useful in their own teams.
