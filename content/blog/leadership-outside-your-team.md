## The boundary is part of the system

A capable team can still struggle when priorities arrive through competing channels. Local improvements help, but they cannot resolve an organisation that asks for incompatible outcomes. Engineering leadership therefore includes making dependencies and trade-offs visible to people who do not share the team’s daily context.

## Translate constraints into decisions

A request for more capacity is difficult to assess without alternatives. Describe what can be delivered with the current constraints, what must wait, and which uncertainty could change the answer. This creates a decision that colleagues can participate in, rather than a technical problem they are expected to accept.

## Relationships are working infrastructure

Cross-team relationships are often described as influence. A more useful framing is shared understanding: who needs to know what, and when? Regular conversations with product, operations and risk can surface conflicting assumptions before they become delivery failures. The aim is not agreement on everything; it is a reliable way to resolve disagreement.

## Keep the team in the conversation

Working beyond team boundaries carries a risk: a leader can become the only route through which context flows. Share decisions and their rationale, invite engineers into relevant discussions, and make commitments visible. A useful test is whether coordination becomes easier when the leader is absent. Leadership should increase the organisation’s ability to cooperate, rather than make cooperation dependent on one person.

## A dependency is also a disagreement waiting to happen

Imagine two teams sharing a platform. One needs a new capability quickly; the other is trying to reduce operational risk. Both can be making sensible decisions. From inside either team, the other can look like the obstacle. Adding a dependency ticket does little to explain why their priorities differ.

The useful conversation starts one level up. Which outcome matters to the organisation, what risk is it prepared to accept, and who can decide? That may sound obvious, yet it is easy to spend weeks negotiating delivery dates before establishing whether the work should happen at all. A leader can help by bringing the competing objectives into the same conversation, without turning a planning disagreement into a contest of personalities.

This hypothetical example also reveals a limit to influence. A good relationship cannot manufacture capacity. Sometimes the responsible result is a clear decision to wait, reduce scope or stop. Getting everyone to say yes is a poor substitute for understanding what that yes will cost.

## Coordination has a cost too

There is a tempting response to every boundary problem: another meeting. Soon the calendar becomes a diagram of the organisation’s unresolved dependencies. Engineers lose focus time, decisions travel through too many intermediaries, and coordination itself becomes the bottleneck.

Before introducing a recurring forum, ask what information it must produce. A decision owner, a shared set of assumptions and an explicit escalation point may achieve more than a standing hour with twelve people. Some dependencies need close collaboration. Others need a stable interface and permission to work independently. Treating them all the same wastes attention.

The Google SRE chapter on communication and collaboration offers a useful perspective on teams operating across organisational boundaries. Its context is particular to SRE, rather than a universal recipe. The broader question it prompts is valuable: does the way people exchange information fit the work they are actually trying to coordinate?

## Make progress visible without claiming all the credit

Work across boundaries can be difficult to explain because its output is often a decision that prevents wasted effort. That does not make it less valuable, but it does make it easy to describe vaguely. Instead of saying that stakeholder alignment improved, describe the choice that became possible. Was an ambiguous dependency given an owner? Did teams agree which outcome would take priority? Was a commitment reduced to something deliverable?

There is a balance here. A leader should make this work legible without treating colleagues as supporting characters in a personal success story. The goal is shared capability. Credit should travel with the people who contributed, and the reasoning should remain available to those who were not in the room.

For anyone leading a team, a useful question is: **which recurring frustration is being produced outside the team’s decision boundary?** Choose one. Trace the decisions behind it. Find the people who see the other side of the problem. There may be a technical improvement to make, but the first useful change might simply be a better conversation with a clearer decision at the end.
